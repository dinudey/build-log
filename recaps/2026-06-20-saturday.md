### Experiment: AI UX Ethics Analyzer

**What it was trying to learn**
Whether Claude can analyze visual and prototype artifacts — UI flows, HTML prototypes, Figma exports — and assess them against AI UX ethics parameters (Trust, Explainability, Transparency, Fairness, Accountability) in a way that's *actionable for UX designers*, not just researchers or compliance teams.

The secondary question was equally specific: can the output meaningfully distinguish between what a designer can act on directly, what requires cross-functional collaboration, and what is simply outside design's remit?

**What was actually built**
A 71KB self-contained HTML prototype exploring four distinct wireframe paradigms for surfacing the same ethics assessment to a designer:

1. **Report Document** — a full narrative output with Socratic summaries, per-pillar breakdowns, a prioritized findings list, and a scope-of-influence appendix
2. **Dashboard Grid** — a quick-scan view with pillar score cards, severity-sorted priority list, and navigation to individual findings
3. **Canvas Annotate** — findings overlaid directly on the assessed UI screens, so the designer sees the flag in context rather than in a separate report
4. **Split Inspector** — annotated screen alongside the full finding detail, side by side

**Non-obvious decisions or tradeoffs**
- **Socratic prompts over prescriptive fixes.** Each finding surfaces a question rather than an instruction — "What does the user understand about why this suggestion appeared?" rather than "Add an explanation." This shifts the interaction from the AI issuing corrections to the AI helping the designer think. It's a meaningful stance on what AI assistance is *for* in a design context.
- **Three-tier scope model, visually encoded.** The prototype encodes designer ownership in color: moss green for things the designer controls directly, ochre for decisions that need cross-team collaboration, clay red for things outside design's scope entirely. This directly answers the secondary research question — and sidesteps the problem of AI feedback that tells you what's wrong without telling you whether you can actually fix it.
- **Four information architectures for the same data.** Building four paradigms rather than one is a deliberate test: the question isn't whether Claude can generate an ethics assessment, but which surface makes it useful enough to integrate into a real design workflow. The output of this experiment is as much a layout hypothesis as a design artifact.

**What it unlocked or proved**
That AI-generated ethics assessments can be structured for *design workflow integration* rather than compliance documentation. The scope-of-influence model is particularly reusable — it's a pattern that belongs in any AI feedback tool where the recipient has partial rather than full control over the outcome.

The four-paradigm approach also proved something about the experiment method itself: the right way to explore AI-native tooling is to prototype the *output* before building the *system*.

---

**Key decisions or tradeoffs**
The biggest tradeoff was scope specificity over generality. The wireframes model a very particular kind of user — a UX designer with cross-functional adjacency to AI/ML work, not a solo practitioner or a researcher. That specificity makes the scope-of-influence model coherent, but it also limits how broadly the output format can generalize without modification.

**Artifact potential**

The specific argument: *most AI feedback tools ignore organizational power — this one maps it explicitly.* Could become a tight artifact on AI-native design tooling practice, particularly the insight that scope-of-influence is a missing layer in nearly every AI feedback surface.
