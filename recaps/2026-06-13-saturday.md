**What I worked on**

**Ethics & Trust Analyzer** (`ethics and trust/`)

The question this experiment was chasing: can Claude actually be useful to a UX designer trying to evaluate their own work for ethical risk — not as a compliance checklist handed down from legal, but as a collaborator that speaks the designer's language and respects their scope of influence?

The five pillars in scope were Trust, Explainability, Transparency, Fairness, and Accountability. But the more interesting design problem turned out to be the second half of the question: given a set of findings, which ones does the designer actually own? What requires product/engineering alignment? What is simply out of design's reach altogether?

What got built was a fully rendered, self-contained HTML prototype (~71KB) with at least seven distinct screens exploring how to surface that analysis. A sample audit produced a score of 42/100 with 40 findings — enough to stress-test whether the output felt alarming or actionable. The screens covered: a long-scroll printable audit report, a pillar-score dashboard grid, a canvas annotation view that maps findings directly onto the uploaded artifact, a split inspector panel, and a close-up on individual AI suggestion cards.

---

**Key decisions or tradeoffs**

**Scope tricolor as a primary design pattern.** Every finding and suggestion is bucketed into one of three ownership zones — "You Own" (moss green), "Collaborate" (ochre), "Out of Scope" (clay red). This is the central design commitment of the prototype: ethics feedback should tell designers what they can move, not just what is wrong. The tradeoff is that inferring scope from a visual artifact requires Claude to make assumptions about team structure that may not hold. But defaulting to showing scope as uncertain still beats defaulting to a flat list where everything looks equally like the designer's problem.

**Four consumption formats for the same analysis.** The prototype doesn't pick one information architecture — it explores a printable report, a dashboard grid, a canvas overlay, and a split inspector. This wasn't indecision; it was the thesis: the hardest design problem here isn't generating the analysis, it's matching the output format to how the designer actually needs to use it (async review, design critique, cross-functional standup, personal audit). No single format handles all four.

**AI suggestion cards with explicit scope tags.** The suggestion surface shows a label like `SUGGESTED FIX · IN YOUR SCOPE` before giving the fix. This is a small interaction decision with a large implication — it frames the AI as an ally helping you pick your battles, not an omniscient auditor. The companion patterns ("Socratic prompt," "Questions to bring to standup," "Collaborators inferred from this artifact") reinforce the same stance.

**Tweaks panel for design option comparison.** An interactive sidebar lets you toggle between design variants inline. This confirms the prototype was never meant as a single static wireframe — it was built as a comparison vehicle, intended to generate a conversation about which format works best before the question is resolved.

---

**What it unlocked**

- Proved that scope attribution is a viable and necessary UX pattern for AI-generated recommendations — not just in ethics tools, but in any context where AI findings span multiple team roles. The tricolor system could generalize to accessibility audits, performance reviews, or any AI feedback loop that crosses ownership boundaries.
- Demonstrated that "format" is itself a first-class design question in AI output design. The four-format exploration surfaces a gap that most AI tool builders skip: they design the analysis but not the consumption context.
- The "questions to bring to standup" output points toward a new AI output category: prompts for human conversations. Claude as meeting prep, not just as auditor. This is a pattern worth expanding.
- The experiment validates the hypothesis enough to justify a second pass — particularly on the canvas annotation format, which is probably the highest-value surface for design team workflows but the least developed in this round.

---

**Loose ends**

- The prototype is a design exploration, not a working tool. The hardest implementation question — how Claude actually reads and evaluates an uploaded visual artifact — is unresolved. The wireframes assume the analysis pipeline exists without building it.
- The scope inference logic (what counts as "You Own" vs. "Collaborate") is hand-waved in the prototype. That's the piece most likely to break down on real artifacts, and probably the most important thing to stress-test next.
- Only one experiment in the repo has any content. The `template` folder is a placeholder. The repo structure suggests an intent to run multiple parallel experiments that hasn't materialized yet.

---

**Artifact potential**

**HIGH — flag for write-up.**

This is a strong candidate for a shareable case study about AI-native design practice. The angle: most AI ethics tooling is built for ML engineers or compliance officers. This experiment asks what it looks like when UX designers are the primary user — with scope attribution, consumption-context awareness, and an output format designed to generate team conversation rather than generate a report to file. The scope tricolor pattern is concrete and reusable. The multi-format exploration makes a genuinely novel point about AI output design that doesn't appear much in current writing on the topic. A write-up that leads with the "what can I actually change?" framing and uses the wireframes as illustration would land well in a design practice or AI product context.

## This week's nudge

*No commits in May or June 2026. Three things worth trying — each doable in 1–2 hours.*

**1. Stress-test the scope inference with a real artifact.**
Take a screenshot or export of an actual product screen (yours or a public app) and prompt Claude to apply the Ethics & Trust Analyzer framework: identify 3–5 findings and classify each as designer-owned, cross-functional, or out of scope. Don't use the wireframe UI — just do it in raw conversation. The goal is to find out whether Claude's scope reasoning holds up on real material, or whether it needs a richer context prompt to make accurate calls. Document what breaks.

**2. Build the "questions to bring to standup" output as a standalone prompt.**
The suggestion card prototype contains a `QUESTIONS TO BRING TO STANDUP` section that's easy to overlook. That framing — Claude as meeting prep tool — is worth developing separately. Write a Claude Code routine that takes a design decision description and outputs 3 questions structured by role: one for the designer, one for the PM, one for the engineer. Test it on 3–4 real decisions you've faced. The artifact here is the prompt pattern, not a UI.

**3. Try the canvas annotation format with a live web prototype.**
Frame 03 of the wireframes (Canvas Annotate) is the most spatially interesting format — findings overlaid on the artifact itself. Build a minimal version: take a single HTML page, use absolute-positioned overlays to annotate 3–5 specific elements with scope-coded findings, and style them with the tricolor system from the wireframe. The goal isn't polish — it's finding out whether annotation-as-layer actually helps a designer understand the findings faster than a report format does. Ship it as a static HTML file.
